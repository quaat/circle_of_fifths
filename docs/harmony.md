# Harmony conventions

The circle pairs each major key with its correctly spelled relative natural minor. Minor Roman numerals are **scale-relative**: i, ii°, III, iv, v, VI, VII. Chromatic raising/lowering is stated explicitly. This differs from notation that writes ♭III/♭VI/♭VII relative to the parallel major.

The diatonic chart uses major or natural-minor triads. Minor V7 and vii°7 use the raised leading tone of harmonic minor and are identified separately. A minor ii–V–i preparation uses iiø7–V7–i. In major, the fully diminished leading-tone seventh also lowers degree 6; it is not the diatonic half-diminished seventh.

Recommendations are contextual examples, not universally interchangeable chords. A **replacement** takes a chord's place; a **preparation** is inserted before a target; a **cadence** demonstrates an approach to tonic. Melody, bass, rhythm, style and voice leading determine whether an example works. Functional labels simplify major-key relationships; natural-minor v and VII are labelled modal rather than equated with a leading-tone dominant.

A tritone substitution here replaces an explicit dominant seventh with another dominant seventh a tritone away. The two guide-tone pitch classes (third and seventh) are checked. For example, G7's B/F are enharmonically Db7's Cb/F. Neither a minor triad nor every diminished chord supports this rule.

The Neapolitan is a chromatic major chord on lowered degree 2, usually in first inversion: Db/F–G7–C. It is not diatonic to parallel natural minor. Parallel-minor borrowing is demonstrated separately by F–Fm–C.
